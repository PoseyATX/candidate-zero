How to Play

A roguelike of Texas retail politics. Easy to learn; the county takes years.

The Shape of It

You are nobody, running for the Texas House. Eight weeks to the primary, six more to the general if you survive it. Before any of that counts, you must make the ballot by the end of week 8 — or the primary goes on without you.

Four Acts

  • Act I — Primary — campaign hand, ballot doors, shop. Ballot or die.
  • Act II — General — GOTV and contrast. Six weeks to November.
  • Act III — Session — legislative motions after a general win.
  • Act IV — Waiting — after a loss, interim weeks. You re-file as the same person.

Identity

First filing: pick who you are, what you run on, and where you file. Those picks stick. Later filings keep the same identity.

The Goal Strip

Under the act banner: what matters · how far you are · what to do next.

Your Week

Each week you get 5 action points plus a separate turf budget (the dashed pips), and a hand of cards. Play until you're out of actions, then End Week.

Cards cost different amounts — a yard sign is cheap, a fish fry is a real day's work. Field plays spend your turf budget first, then campaign AP, so working the ground doesn't eat the week you need to make the ballot.

You also get two cuts a week to throw away cards you can't use, and a heat meter that builds as plays land. Both live in the strip at the top of the screen, and both reset when the week turns.

Reading a Card

  • The face shows title, art, and cost only.
  • First tap opens a full brief — story, risk, and odds in plain English; Play commits.
  • Field plays pick a ground. Higher rival opposition makes that ground harder — the brief shows the tax.
  • Dimmed cards can't be played yet — the brief says why.

Risk

  • Safe — never a disaster.
  • Standard — honest variance.
  • Volatile — swings both ways.

Cutting a Card

You get two cuts a week (the diamond pips up top). Open a card you can't use and take Cut it — draw another: it goes back into the deck and you draw a replacement. Cuts cost no action points, and they don't carry over — an unused cut is a wasted one.

One exception: before you're on the ballot, the game won't let you cut away your last way onto it.

Heat, and Pressing It

Every play that lands builds heat. Any play that fails wipes it. Heat does nothing on its own — it only matters when you spend it.

On a card's brief, Press spends your whole streak on that one play: better odds, and a wider chance of disaster to pay for them. The payoff climbs steeply, so a streak of four is worth far more than four streaks of one — but you're wagering a streak you might rather keep. Safe cards are the exception: pressing one buys the odds and still can't produce a disaster.

The dice are never rigged for you or against you. Pressing moves the odds before the roll; it never touches the roll itself, and losing never earns you a discount.

Practising a Card

Between acts you're offered a draft. Sometimes a slot offers to practise a card you already run instead of handing you a new one — better odds on it, or one less action point, for the rest of the run. A practised card carries a ✦ on its face.

That's the real question the draft asks: a wider deck, or a sharper one.

Holding the Seat

If you win, Session is a different game. Passing your bill is the prize, but the seat itself is kept at home: do casework every week. Skip it and your district standing bleeds, a challenger circles, and you can lose the seat you just won even while your bill is doing fine.

The odds printed on a card are not the same thing as the value of playing it. Session is where that difference bites hardest.

The Two Doors to the Ballot

Labor: petition signatures. Money: the filing fee. Most runs die at this door.

Losing Well

A run ending is not a reset. On a loss you take an interim path, bank contacts and name, then re-file as the same person.

Where do you work it?

You = your rapport on that ground. Rival = opposition presence — higher rival lowers field odds. Same ground twice in a week yields half rapport.
